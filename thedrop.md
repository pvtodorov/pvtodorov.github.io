## Wednesday 16 September, 10:30

- **[Climate Risk & the Insurability Crisis](https://luma.com/enpf9hbi)**, Ripple Area 8, hosted by [Laura Fox](https://www.linkedin.com/in/lauraafox/) ([Streetlife Ventures](https://www.linkedin.com/company/streetlifevc)) and [Michael Langguth](https://www.linkedin.com/in/langguthmichael/) ([Carbon13](https://uk.linkedin.com/company/carbon13)). Climate risk has stopped being a forecast and become a line on the balance sheet. The numbers they open with are stark: over $12 trillion of US home value projected at risk over the next thirty years, over €800 billion of European climate losses since 1980, only about 25% of European losses covered, and nearly 40 million American homes now facing reduced insurance availability as carriers retreat from high-risk markets. The framing that makes it more than a housing story is that the protection gap lands on corporate balance sheets, public finances and infrastructure too, and that homes are the single largest share of household savings at around 40%. The tension they put at the end is the one nobody wants to chair: significant changes to the built environment versus simply accepting that some areas become unlivable.

- **[What Nature Markets Need to Scale](https://luma.com/wttd4uva)**, Ripple Area 6, hosted by [Ed Thorne](https://www.linkedin.com/in/ethorne1/) ([Sand River](https://uk.linkedin.com/company/sand-river)) with Kevin Webb. The analogy does the work here. "The clean energy transition didn't happen because of wind farms", they write, "it happened because of batteries, turbines and PV cells." So what is the component layer for nature. Their argument is that nature markets in 2026 sit roughly where clean energy sat in the early 2010s, with companies now building supply chain transparency systems, biological inputs that compete with synthetics on economics rather than virtue, tradeable nature assets and transition risk tools. The three questions are which institutional capital bottleneck breaks first, whether regenerative can outcompete extractive on price alone, and what the infrastructure actually looks like by 2030.

## Wednesday 16 September, 11:30

- **[Conventional agriculture: the efficiency myth](https://luma.com/lx7iowcj)**, Ripple Area 10, hosted by [Sanne Bootsma](https://nl.linkedin.com/in/sanne-bootsma) ([DOEN Ventures](https://www.linkedin.com/company/doen-participaties/)) and [Erica van Eeghen](https://nl.linkedin.com/in/ericavaneeghen) ([VP Capital](https://www.linkedin.com/company/vpcapital-familyoffice)). The claim is that modern farming optimised for short-term efficiency while quietly degrading the systems it depends on, through fossil-based inputs, degraded soils and inefficient land use. The most useful specific in the whole programme sits here: soil regeneration runs on a 7 to 10 year cycle against fund structures of 3 to 5 years, which is a structural mismatch rather than a lack of conviction. They also float healthcare and insurance as plausible funders of food system resilience, on the logic that they carry the downstream cost.

- **[European Sovereignty Playbook](https://luma.com/bg7zzwf9)**, Ripple Area 5, hosted by [Maxi Pethö-Schramm](https://de.linkedin.com/in/anneke-maxi-peth%C3%B6-schramm) ([HV Capital](https://www.linkedin.com/company/hvcapital)) and [Birthe Ross](https://de.linkedin.com/in/birthe-ross/de) ([SPRIND](https://www.linkedin.com/company/sprind/)). About building "European primes" across energy, critical minerals, semiconductors, robotics, defence and compute, meaning companies whose significance to the continent exceeds any conventional venture outcome. The practical content is cap table design for a company destined to be a critical national asset, plus how to win government contracts and use non-dilutive funding without losing speed. The paradox they name is the honest one: companies essential to European independence still need global capital and global markets to reach competitive scale, and sovereignty is still funded nationally while the ambition is continental.

- **[Natural Solutions: Valuable or Money Wasted?](https://luma.com/m6jayd88)**, Ripple Area 4, hosted by [Jeff Johnson](https://www.linkedin.com/in/jeffreyjohnson/) ([B Capital](https://www.linkedin.com/company/b-capital-group)) and [Kyle Teamey](https://www.linkedin.com/in/kyle-teamey-9a53197) ([RA Capital Planetary Health](https://www.linkedin.com/company/ra-capital-management-llc)). The sceptical counterweight to the nature markets session, and worth attending as a pair with it. They point at marginal abatement cost studies showing emissions can be cut far more cheaply through building insulation and vehicle emissions programmes than through natural solutions, then ask why there are so few commercially successful nature-based businesses at all, and whether the honest answer is that this belongs to philanthropy and government rather than venture.

- **[Unlocking Hidden Innovation](https://luma.com/wuvj8a8s)**, Ripple Area 8, hosted by [Andrea Siciliani](https://www.linkedin.com/in/andreasiciliani/) ([Techenergy Ventures](https://www.linkedin.com/company/techenergyventures)) and [Leonardo Massa](https://it.linkedin.com/in/leonardomassa) ([MITO Technology](https://www.linkedin.com/company/mito-technology)). Their line is that universities and corporate R&D labs are "sitting on a goldmine of innovation that never reaches the market, buried under slow IP deals, misaligned incentives, and ecosystems that don't talk to each other." The evidence they cite is all from 2025: 76 university spin-outs crossed $1B valuations, VCs deployed over €8B into European deep tech, and corporates including Bosch and Tenova launched structured spin-out programmes. The barriers are different on each side: drawn-out equity negotiations for academics, internal incentives that never reward commercialisation for corporate R&D. The actual debate is whether the bigger unlock comes from fixing each side separately or from connecting them into one ecosystem.

## Wednesday 16 September, 14:15

- **[Amid Market Backlash: Pitching Climate 2026](https://luma.com/kzx18ncg)**, Ripple Area 3, hosted by [Philip Specht](https://www.linkedin.com/in/philip-specht-b75972a3/) ([AENU](https://www.linkedin.com/company/aenu)) and [Hugo Rauch](https://www.linkedin.com/in/hugorauch/) (New Wave and [Climate Club](https://www.linkedin.com/company/climateclub-cc/)). Does the climate movement have a marketing problem. Over the last two to three years political support has weakened in key markets, conflict and geopolitics have pushed climate down the agenda, and the AI wave is absorbing most of the tech ecosystem's attention. Separately, capital has become more selective, LP scrutiny has intensified, and many climate startups report longer sales cycles and a harder fundraising environment. The session works through how climate is currently pitched to founders, VCs, LPs and corporates, what still lands with each, and whether the sector should move beyond the term "climate tech" altogether.

- **[Can Robots secure Europe's Food System?](https://luma.com/8yda2rik)**, Ripple Area 6, hosted by [Julius Strauss](https://www.linkedin.com/in/julius-strauss/) ([FoodLabs](https://www.linkedin.com/company/foodlabsvc/)) and [Philip Stark](https://www.linkedin.com/in/philipstark/) ([Oyster Bay](https://www.linkedin.com/company/oysterbayvc/)). Opens with "Hormuz, Ukraine, AGI - threats to food supply are increasing, and Europe needs to make more of what we eat. But with labour costs rising and immigration rules tightening, can robots ride to the rescue?" The case is that labour, input and transport costs are all rising while robots get dramatically smarter and cheaper, so the question becomes which shortages are most urgent and which crops are most robotics-ready. The sentence that stayed with me is "Food sovereignty is never a priority until it's too late." There is a dual-use defence thread running underneath.

- **[Food to Fashion to Food: Bio Closed-Loop](https://luma.com/nphtlr7p)**, Ripple Area 4, hosted by [Nic Gorini](https://www.linkedin.com/in/nicgorini/) ([Spin Ventures](https://uk.linkedin.com/company/spin-ventures)) and [Michelle de Rijk](https://www.linkedin.com/in/michelle-de-rijk-bba96661/) ([Fair Capital Impact Fund](https://www.linkedin.com/company/fair-capital-impact-fund/)). Closed-loop materials treated as an asset class rather than a sustainability story. Europe already holds both hard assets the model needs: the EU throws away over 58 million tonnes of food a year worth around €132bn, and its textile and clothing sector runs to roughly 192,000 companies, 1.3 million workers and about €167bn in turnover, inside a bioeconomy valued at €2.7tn. The questions that matter commercially are which biomaterial pathways can deliver venture-scale returns before 2030, how feedstock aggregation locks in a durable input cost advantage, and which processing and recovery infrastructure drives asset-level returns as textile collection volumes rise under the 2025 EU rules. Their framing of the tension is insetting and real reduction versus offsetting and market-driven apparent impact.

- **[Hardware & Biology: Durable Moats Beyond Code](https://luma.com/gc1tdw4k)**, Ripple Area 7, hosted by Max Lebeau ([Positron Ventures](https://www.linkedin.com/company/positron-ventures)) and [Isabel Zhang](https://www.linkedin.com/in/isabel-zhang/) ([HCVC](https://www.linkedin.com/company/hardware-club)). The shortest page of the lot and the sharpest thesis: "As AI commoditizes software features overnight, hardware and biological systems offer structural defensibility that code cannot match." Manufacturing bottlenecks, regulatory pathways and proprietary datasets become the moat, and the price of that moat is capital intensity, regulatory complexity and physical constraint. Their phrase for it is that the next decade of defensibility lives in atoms and cells, not algorithms.

## Wednesday 16 September, 16:15

- **[De-risking the Dragon, embracing the Elephant](https://luma.com/8mf952hr)**, Ripple Area 6, hosted by [Divya Murthy](https://de.linkedin.com/in/divyabn) ([Oyster Bay](https://www.linkedin.com/company/oysterbayvc/)) and [Mark Kahn](https://ae.linkedin.com/in/mark-kahn-20490a) ([Omnivore](https://in.linkedin.com/company/omnivore-partners)). Much the bluntest page in the programme. China "might be the global leader in climate tech, but it will inevitably treat Europe as a vassal state", America "has lost the plot and become a hegemonic predator state", and China "wants to bury the EU in exports, completing the deindustrialization of Europe". The constructive half is India as the balanced partner, on the back of the EU-India trade treaty that von der Leyen called "the mother of all deals", covering how European startups engage India as customer, supplier and research partner, and how to answer the dated objections about it being chaotic, corrupt or inefficient.

- **[Food investment - the next wave (the Tsunami)](https://luma.com/thedrop-buu0)**, Ripple Area 11, hosted by [Johan Jörgensen](https://www.linkedin.com/in/johanjorgensen/) ([Sweden Foodtech](https://www.linkedin.com/company/swedenfoodtech)) and [Björn Öste](https://www.linkedin.com/in/bj%C3%B6rn-%C3%B6ste-b185551/) (Good Idea). The maximalist take, and it does not hedge: "Food will be bigger than AI - remember where you heard it first!" The argument is that we know almost nothing about food and human biology, that the science is now moving at breakneck speed, and that what is coming is a Copernican shift rather than an improvement. The forcing events they name are "the dry and flaming summer of -26" and the GLP-1 revolution. The page warns you should be reasonably versed in science and open to changing your worldview, which is a fair warning to print.

- **[Infra-tech: New definitions & funding modes](https://luma.com/53sqyovx)**, Ripple Area 4, hosted by [Shakil Karim](https://www.linkedin.com/in/shakil-karim-5080b412a/) ([Actia Capital Partners](https://www.linkedin.com/company/actia-partners)) and [Toba Spiegel](https://at.linkedin.com/in/tobaspiegel) ([Loom Ventures](https://at.linkedin.com/company/loomvc)). Compressed into one line: "AI demand > supply > planetary boundaries with current infra." The dividing lines between infrastructure categories are blurring, and as they blur the business models and the funding modes change with them. The page keeps it deliberately open: what the emerging models are, and to what extent new ways of supporting these companies are appearing on the capital side. Framed as founders comparing scaling routes and investors comparing notes on where the category now starts and stops.

- **[Is Resilience a Lie We Tell Ourselves?](https://luma.com/070moc9e)**, Ripple Area 9, hosted by [Till Stenzel](https://www.linkedin.com/in/drtillstenzel/) ([SET Ventures](https://www.linkedin.com/company/setventures)) and [Katharina Beitz](https://www.linkedin.com/in/thedigitalkatharinabeitz/) ([Vireo Ventures](https://www.linkedin.com/company/vireo-ventures)). The sharpest framing of the two days: "Decentralization removes the honeypot but creates millions of entry points." Every solar inverter, home battery and smart meter widens the attack surface, and they cite the planned cyberattack on distributed energy assets in Poland in late 2025 as the point where this stopped being hypothetical. Their claim is that the gap between deployment speed and security readiness is "widening - not closing", that most energy tech investors still do not look at cybersecurity in diligence, and they leave open the question they think matters most, which is where the line now sits between civilian infrastructure and a strategic asset once energy assets become targets of cyber warfare.

- **[Mine or Be Mined](https://luma.com/vjr3v0xh)**, Ripple Area 2, hosted by [Lasse Köhler](https://www.linkedin.com/in/lassekohler/) ([EIFO](https://www.linkedin.com/company/eifo)) and [Johan Attby](https://www.linkedin.com/in/johanattby/) ([Norrsken Evolve](https://www.linkedin.com/company/norrsken-evolve)). Three doors for Europe on critical minerals: strengthen domestic extraction and processing, deepen partnerships with trusted allies, or invest hard in technologies that reduce the need for scarce materials at all. Set against recent rare earth export controls and the implementation of the EU Critical Raw Materials Act. The honest problem they put on the table is that extraction and refining are "capital-intensive, complex, and subject to long development cycles, making them a challenging fit for traditional venture capital", which turns the session into a question about sovereign and philanthropic capital sitting alongside VC in the stack.

- **[Ocean tech: too early or mis-funded?](https://luma.com/5b5vg4tm)**, Ripple Area 5, hosted by [Jerome Ternynck](https://www.linkedin.com/in/jerometernynck/) ([Rypples](https://www.linkedin.com/company/rypples/)) and [James Lindsay](https://www.linkedin.com/in/jamesarthurlindsay/) ([Builders Vision](https://www.linkedin.com/company/builders-vision)). Ocean ecosystems are "one of the biggest buffers we have" and remain chronically underfunded, while capital flows into energy, food and nature systems that only make sense if planetary boundaries hold. Their sentence for it is that the gap between what the science demands and where capital goes "is starting to look less like caution and more like a mis-pricing we can't afford". The real question is where the bottleneck sits, in technology readiness, patient capital or plain investor familiarity. The page says plainly this is not an intro session but a debate for people ready to take a position.

- **[What future will the boldest aspire to build?](https://luma.com/0cq5mh5f)**, Ripple Area 8, hosted by [Anna Ottosson](https://se.linkedin.com/in/annaottosson) ([Mudcake](https://se.linkedin.com/company/mudcake)) and [Jonny Everett](https://www.linkedin.com/in/jonnyeverett/) ([Marble](https://www.linkedin.com/company/marble-climate)). The talent question, posed as an open one rather than a panel. The coming decade brings both the consequences of the planetary crisis and the implications of AGI, and the risk they name is that the sharpest people spend it "heads down building wealth without a purpose", drawn by "the lure of building AI startups without any intention of making the world better". The premise worth arguing with is that the old assumption, that top talent naturally gravitates to important problems, has quietly stopped being true.
